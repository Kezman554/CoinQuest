2026-08-20 — Session C: schema and the first migration

Tables (app/models/): chore_definitions, chore_instances, weeks,
settlement_lines, waivers, earnings_ledger, savings_ledger.

The separation the scheme turns on:
  - A definition is a rule and will be edited. An instance is working data
    and may point at one freely.
  - A settled week keeps its own figures, and settlement_lines keep their own
    copy of the chore's name, category, cadence and amount as they stood at
    the moment of settlement. source_definition_id is provenance only,
    nullable, ON DELETE SET NULL, and never read to work out money.

Enforced by the database, not by the application remembering:
  - Triggers refuse any change to a closed week's figures or status, any
    update or delete on either ledger, and any edit to a settlement line.
    paid_at and deposited_pence stay writable, because payday follows
    settlement.
  - CHECKs: a week starts on a Sunday (strftime %w) and runs seven days; a
    closed week carries all four figures and they add up; a voided week pays
    zero; no amount anywhere is negative; only a withdrawal is negative in
    savings and the balance never goes below zero; a weekly_count chore
    states its count and no other cadence may; a waiver fills in the columns
    its scope allows; a reward states its reason.
  - Partial unique indexes: one instance per chore per day, or per chore per
    week for the week-scoped cadences.

Alembic initialised at app/migrations with the URL supplied from the
environment through app.config, never from alembic.ini. render_as_batch is on
because SQLite cannot alter a column. alembic check reports no drift, and
downgrade base leaves an empty file.

One conflict found and resolved: ON DELETE SET NULL on a settlement line's
provenance column fires an UPDATE, which the blanket append-only trigger
blocked — making a definition undeletable once settled, and the SET NULL dead
configuration. The trigger now freezes every column that carries the money or
the story and permits exactly one change: provenance being cleared, never
repointed.

Instants are stored UTC and returned aware via a UtcDateTime type; a naive
datetime is refused rather than assumed. Local days are separate Date columns,
computed in Europe/London by app.services.calendar.

Tests: 138 pass. Schema tests build the database by running the real migration
against an empty file, not create_all, because the triggers exist only in the
migration. The central case asserts that renaming, repricing, recategorising
and withdrawing a chore leaves a settled week's stored figures untouched, and
that deleting the definition outright does too.

2026-08-20 — Session D: generating a week's assessable instances

app/services/instances.py. Pure: it reads no clock, writes nothing, and takes
a Period, the definitions and the waivers.

  - DAILY produces one instance per day the week contains — seven, including
    in the 169-hour October week.
  - WEEKLY_COUNT produces n instances tied to no day, numbered 1..n so each
    is separately claimable.
  - WEEKLY_CONDITION produces no instance at all. It appears in the plan as a
    deferred judgement, made once at settlement: a condition held all week
    cannot be assessed before the week has finished happening.
  - ONE_OFF and EVENT are not derived from a week. Both are created by a
    parent when they happen and neither is predictable from a definition.

Waivers: a waived day removes that day's daily instances; a chore waived for
the week is removed whatever its cadence; a day waiver outside the week or a
chore waiver for another week is ignored. A week-long condition is not made
of days, so waiving one does not reduce it.

The scaling table is data, in WEEKLY_COUNT_BANDS: (up_to_days_waived,
reduce_by, waives_entirely). 0-2 full count, 3-4 one occasion off, 5-7 not
assessed. Every function takes the bands as an argument, and a test replaces
the whole table with a harsher one and asserts the logic is untouched. Days
outside the table raise rather than being guessed at.

Schema change this card forced (revision b743007f1216): the first migration
allowed one week-scoped instance per chore per week, which is right for a
condition and wrong for a chore due three times a week. Added a `sequence`
column and moved the partial unique index to

triggers in place.

sync_week_instances writes a plan to the database. It never touches an

2026-08-20 — Session F: misses, recovery, and the best assignment

app/services/recovery.py. Pure apart from one writer.

Assessment is against the requirement, never a count of rows. Requirement
comes from the week's plan; the question is whether that many were confirmed,
and shortfall is floored at zero. A waiver applied after work was confirmed
leaves a confirmed instance the plan no longer asks for, and that surplus is
harmless: it cannot go negative and cannot buy off a miss elsewhere. Both are
tested.

Misses: a parent-marked miss is definite and fills the shortfall first; the
rest are inferred at settlement. If a parent marked more missed than the week
ended up short of — which a later waiver can cause — the extra ones simply do
not become misses.

An inferred miss has no author. record_inferred_misses writes
miss_origin=inferred_at_settlement and clears authorised_by, because if the
child had a claim rejected on that instance earlier in the week the rejecting
parent's name is still sitting there and would read as though they had ruled
it missed. The rejection survives in rejected_at/rejection_count, which is
where that fact belongs. Revision 82826e03b64d adds miss_origin with three
CHECKs: a miss says how it arose, an inferred miss has no author, and a
parent-marked one names the parent.

Recovery eligibility is derived from ON_DEMAND_CADENCES in enums.py — daily,
weekly_count, one_off. A weekly condition cannot be started on Thursday and an
event is not the child's to decide. No chore name appears in the rule.

The optimiser evaluates every lawful assignment (subsets of completed,
spendable bonus chores up to the cap) and takes the highest total, with
deterministic tie-breaking because the figure is about to be stored forever.
Two things the obvious answer gets wrong, both tested: spending a 300p bonus
when a 100p one would do, and spending anything at all when the misses exceed
the cap.

Discovered while testing: recovering is not always worth doing. Two misses
rescued with 400p of bonus chores to save 350p of chore pay is a 50p loss, and
the optimiser correctly declines. Two of my own tests asserted otherwise and
were wrong.

Also fixed here: one_off and event chores have no plan-derived requirement, so
a completed one-off bonus was invisible to the assessment. For those two
cadences the instances a parent created are the requirement
(WEEK_DERIVED_CADENCES marks the difference).

Appendment: a weekly condition ignores the reduction band — there is no
fraction of "kept tidy all week" to take off — but honours the waives-entirely
band at the same threshold as the counts.

Tests: 236 pass, including the card's three weeks (more bonuses than misses,
more misses than the cap, and greedy paying less than best) and a permutation
test asserting chore ordering never changes the figure.

Open question for settlement: the chore pay is treated as one pot, paid in
full only if every basic requirement was met or recovered, on the strength of
"exceeding the cap fails the chore pay".

2026-08-20 — Session G: proposing and closing a week

app/services/settlement.py

  propose() computes what an open week is worth and writes nothing. It can be
  called by a dashboard tile every five minutes and change nothing; a test
  snapshots the whole database around three calls and asserts equality. In
  particular untouched instances stay untouched — they become misses at
  settlement and not before.

  settle() takes the figure the parent actually read, as agreed_total_pence,
  and refuses if the week no longer proposes it. Storing forever an amount
  nobody agreed to is the one mistake that cannot be corrected afterwards.
  It records the inferred misses, links each spent bonus instance to the miss
  it covered via recovered_instance_id, writes a SettlementLine per assessed
  chore, writes the week's four figures, and writes one earnings entry.

  void() closes a week at zero, keeps every instance exactly as it is, and
  requires a reason.

  propose() refuses a week that is not open. That refusal is the mechanism
  behind "no code path recomputes a settled period", not merely the intention:
  there is no route from a closed week back to a current definition.

app/routers/weeks.py — GET /api/weeks, GET /api/weeks/{id} (stored figures if
closed, proposal if open), GET /api/weeks/{id}/proposal, and POST .../settle
and .../void. Reading needs no PIN; closing always does.

  "Base" is read as the chore pay the week put at stake — everything the basic

  Voiding "does not touch rewards already earned": the week's own figures must

2026-08-20 — Session G addendum: the base allowance, and voided rewards

Two corrections, both from the scheme document rather than from the code.

§1 pays a base allowance every week regardless of how the chores went. That
had been collapsed into what I was calling "base", which was actually the
§2 chore pay at stake. Revision 4e720209aaba renames settled_basic_pence to
settled_chore_pay_pence and adds settled_base_pence beside it; a week's total
is now base + chore pay + bonuses + rewards. WEEKLY_BASE_PENCE is
configuration, defaulting to 100. The proposal now carries base_pence,
chore_pay_at_stake_pence and chore_pay_pence as three distinct figures.

§11: a voided week loses the base, the chore pay and the bonuses — the three
things it names — and rewards keep settling through the ordinary path. The
schema demanded a voided total of zero, which had forced void() to write
rewards to the earnings ledger as a special case. That was a double-payment
waiting to happen: a void is liftable until the week settles, and a reward
written at void-time would be paid again at settlement with an append-only
ledger unable to take it back. The constraint now zeroes the three components
instead, and void() writes reward settlement lines the same way settle() does.

Two things worth knowing for the next migration touching `weeks`:

  - A SQLite batch rebuild recreates the table and silently drops any trigger
    on it. This revision drops and recreates both weeks triggers explicitly,
    and they name the renamed column anyway.
  - An empty batch_alter_table block does not rebuild even with
    recreate="always"; it needs an operation in it. There is a deliberate
    no-op alter_column doing that job, with a comment saying so.

Also: Bash's /tmp and Python's /tmp are different directories on Windows, so a
manual database reset from the shell was silently doing nothing. Not a code
problem, but it wasted a cycle.

2026-08-20 — Session P: attempt limiting on the PIN

app/services/lockout.py. In-memory, per source address, cooling off on its
own. PIN_ATTEMPT_LIMIT (5) and PIN_COOL_OFF_SECONDS (300) are configuration.

The trade-off, decided rather than avoided: counting per source means a device
that guesses wrong locks only itself, so nobody can lock the parent out of
their own app by guessing wrong on purpose — which a single global counter
would allow, with no administrator to appeal to. What it costs is that an
attacker with several devices, or one that can change its address, gets a
fresh budget for each. That turns seconds into a long evening rather than
making guessing impossible, which for this app is the right end of the trade.

The address comes from the socket. Nothing the caller says about itself is
read — a limiter keyed on a claim limits nobody. Behind a reverse proxy every
request would arrive from the proxy, so this degrades to a global counter with
the lockout risk that implies; that is the point at which somebody has to
decide which proxy header to trust. Written up in the module docstring.

The lockout is checked before the PIN is looked at, so a correct guess cannot
open the window early. A wrong PIN is still told nothing; a lockout is told
plainly how long remains, because the likeliest reader is a parent who
mistyped. Retry-After carries the same number.

Found while testing: alembic's fileConfig disables every existing logger by
default, so running a migration in-process silenced coinquest.authorisation —
the logger whose entire job is recording failed attempts. env.py now passes
disable_existing_loggers=False.

Also: the limiter is process-global by design, so tests reset it between
cases via an autouse fixture. Without that, one test's wrong PIN locked out
every test after it.

Tests: 331 pass, with a fake clock so the suite never waits. Verified live
against a running server: four wrong PINs, then 429 with "Try again in 10
seconds", a correct PIN refused while cooling off, the stated wait counting
down, and the correct PIN accepted at ten seconds without a restart. The log
shows each failure and the lockout.

2026-08-20 — Session P revision: the cooling-off escalates

Per-source counting stays and the limit stays at 5. The cooling-off now starts
at 30 seconds, doubles on each consecutive lockout from the same source, caps
at 900, and resets to the start on a correct PIN.
PIN_COOL_OFF_START_SECONDS and PIN_COOL_OFF_MAX_SECONDS are configuration.

The reason, which is not the one I had written down: the kitchen wall screen is
a single source used by the child and the parents both — it is where he claims
and where they confirm — so on the surface that matters most, "a guesser locks
only itself" means he locks his parents out of their own app. A flat five
minutes made the ordinary case (a mistype, a bored nine-year-old) expensive and
the hostile case cheap: 5 guesses per 300s is about 1,440 a day from one
address, the whole four-digit space inside a week. Escalating inverts that. A
mistype costs half a minute; sustained guessing reaches the 15-minute ceiling
after six lockouts and needs over 20 days for the keyspace. Both numbers are
asserted in a test rather than left as a claim in a comment.

Waiting a cooling-off out clears the failure count but not the lockout count,
so the doubling cannot be reset by simply serving each one. Only a correct PIN
clears it.

Tests: 341 pass. Verified live with a 4-second start and a 16-second cap:
4 -> 8 -> 16 -> 16, then a correct PIN, then back to 4. The log names the
lockout number each time.

2026-08-20 — Phase 2 close: wording, a required reason, and voids

1. Refusals are written for a person. "was missed 1 times this week" becomes
   "was missed once this week", a miss that never happened says so rather than
   reporting zero, and counts read "1 miss" or "2 misses". The lockout message
   had the same fault — "Try again in 1 seconds" — and is fixed with it.

2. An override that settles below optimum_total_pence must carry a non-empty
   reason. Enforced in settle() and by a CHECK (revision 3c1f6a2d9b74), since
   "a settled week explains itself" is exactly the rule a later caller forgets.
   An override that costs nothing needs no reason: there is no difference to
   explain. A blank or whitespace reason counts as none.

   Ordering matters here and was wrong at first: the agreed-figure check now
   runs before the reason check. Somebody agreeing the wrong number has not
   read the right proposal, and asking them to justify a choice they have not
   seen is no help.

3. A void takes no assignment. §11 zeroes the base, the chore pay and the
   bonuses, so there is nothing for a make-good to rescue, and accepting one
   would record a deliberate choice that changed nothing. Sending an override

2026-08-21 — Session J: the parent view

The queue, what a batch does, the acts, and a closed week that reads as closed.

app/services/review.py — the batch logic, lifted out of the claims router
because two callers need it now and they must not drift apart. The commit
applies it for real; the parent view asks what it *would* do first. A preview
computed by different code from the commit is not a preview, it is a second
opinion, and the moment the two disagree the figure a parent agreed to is not
the figure that happened.

app/services/parent.py — pending(), and preview().

  preview() answers the question a parent actually has in front of a list of
  ticks: what does agreeing to this do? It applies the batch for real inside a
  SAVEPOINT, proposes each affected week from the result, and rolls the
  savepoint back in a finally. Applying it is the only way to be certain the
  preview and the commit agree — they are then the same code over the same
  data — and the rollback is what makes that safe.

  What comes back is per week and in the week's terms: misses before and
  after, which bonus chore is about to be spent covering which miss, whether
  the chore pay is rescued or lost, and the total either side. "7 items" is a
  fact about the screen. None of the above is derivable from it.

  The author recorded during a preview is "preview", not "parent". Nothing
  written under it survives the savepoint, and using the real string would put
  the record one bug away from being unable to tell a rehearsal from the
  thing itself.

app/routers/parent.py — GET /api/parent/queue and POST
/api/parent/review/preview. Neither carries a PIN. The preview writes and
then throws the write away, and what it exposes is what a proposal already
exposes: what a week would be worth. The PIN guards the act, not the
arithmetic. Confirming for real is still POST /api/claims/review, one PIN,
one transaction, all or nothing.

app/routers/waivers.py — new; there was no way to record a waiver over HTTP
at all. POST re-plans the week it lands in through sync_week_instances, which
removes an occasion the plan no longer wants only while it is still
UNTOUCHED. Waiving Monday after the child's claim was confirmed leaves the
claim standing. A waiver is not a way in through the back door to deleting
somebody's confirmed work. Refused for a closed week: a closed week is not
re-planned, its figures are stored.

Savings gained the two endpoints the parent view needed: POST
/api/savings/withdrawals, and POST /api/savings/reconcile.

  Reconcile records nothing, and that is the design rather than an omission.
  The ledger is append-only with four entry types, none of which means "the
  books were wrong". A fifth would make the balance agree while destroying
  the only information the difference carries — that something happened to
  the account nobody wrote down — and the monthly match is computed from the
  lowest balance a month reached, so an invented adjustment silently moves
  money the scheme is about to pay a match on. Instead the response names the
  difference and which existing act closes it: money missing is a withdrawal
  nobody logged; money extra has no act yet, and it says so plainly rather
  than pretending otherwise.

A real gap found while testing, and fixed. A parent-entered reward is an
earnings-ledger entry, not a chore instance, so it never appears in

frontend/: parentApi.ts, parentWords.ts, ParentView.tsx, and
components/parent/.

  The PIN is asked for per submission by a dialog that first states what is
  about to happen, and is kept nowhere afterwards. It lives in that
  component's own state, is cleared before the request is awaited, and the
  component is unmounted when the act finishes. No unlocked mode, no
  "remember for five minutes", no header set for later. The API refuses these
  requests without it regardless — this is not what makes the app safe — but
  a screen that stayed unlocked on a kitchen wall the child walks past would
  make the server-side check irrelevant in practice.

  Ruling on the queue decides nothing: it marks items. The consequence is
  fetched, then the batch is submitted once. Changing any ruling clears the
  consequence, because a stale one describes a batch nobody is about to send.

  parentWords.ts phrases consequences and never calculates one. If it did
  arithmetic of its own the sentence and the outcome could disagree, which is
  the single failure this whole design exists to rule out.

  Closed weeks render with no controls at all. Nothing in the app can edit a

2026-08-21 — Session K: the summary endpoint

GET /api/summary. Seven fields, 195 bytes on the wire, unauthenticated,
written for a tile on somebody else's dashboard rather than for a page.

  week_start, week_end, status, projected_total_pence, projected_total,
  recovery_outstanding, recovery_deadline, days_remaining

app/services/summary.py. Derived from week_view.build rather than computed
beside it. If this said a recovery was outstanding while the kitchen screen
said the week was fine, the tile would be worse than no tile at all — so
there is one definition of "outstanding" and this reads it.

Four decisions, all of them consequences of what reads this:

  1. The figure is the payable total, rewards included: the tile answers "how
     is he doing", not "what will a parent agree to on Sunday". Those two are
     different numbers and Session J made the difference explicit.

  2. days_remaining is always present, outstanding or not. The week runs
     Sunday to Saturday in Europe/London and contains a clock change twice a
     year; a consumer counting days from a date string gets that wrong every
     autumn and never finds out. It goes through calendar.elapsed and is
     floored at zero, so the last day reads 0 rather than a negative.

  3. A week nothing has happened in has no row, and the endpoint answers
     anyway rather than 404ing. A tile that errors every Sunday morning until
     somebody opens the app is a tile nobody trusts by the third week. The
     figure reported is the base allowance, which is not a stand-in: it is
     exactly what propose() returns for a freshly opened week, since
     everything untouched is a prospective miss and the chore pay fails.
     status is "not_started" so a consumer can tell the difference, and asking
     brings no week into being — it is a GET and it writes nothing.

  4. A closed week reads from its own stored columns, and nothing is
     outstanding in a week that is over.

recovery_deadline is the only nullable field, and it is null for exactly one
reason: there is nothing outstanding to have a deadline. Everything else is
present every time, so a tile never has to branch on which keys arrived.

Nothing about authorisation and nothing chore-level, asserted rather than
assumed: a test greps the response body for "pin", "parent", "locked",
"attempt", "instance", "chore" and the names of the chores in the fixture.
The tests assert the whole key set as a set, not the fields they happen to
care about — a key quietly added or renamed is precisely the change that
breaks a consumer nobody in this repository can see.
